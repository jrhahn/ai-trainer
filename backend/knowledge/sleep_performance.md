# Sleep and Athletic Performance

## Why Sleep Is the #1 Recovery Tool

Sleep is the most powerful recovery intervention available to athletes — more impactful than nutrition timing, massage, compression, cold therapy, or any supplement. During sleep, the body performs the bulk of its cellular repair, hormonal restoration, and neural consolidation. Sleep deprivation of even one night measurably impairs endurance performance, reaction time, decision-making, and pain tolerance.

## The Science of Sleep Stages

Human sleep cycles through ~90-minute repeating stages:

| Stage | Type | Duration/Cycle | Athletic Relevance |
|-------|------|---------------|-------------------|
| N1 | Light NREM | 5–10 min | Transition; minimal recovery value |
| N2 | Light NREM | 20–25 min | Memory consolidation begins |
| N3 | Deep NREM (slow-wave) | 20–40 min (early night) | Peak GH release, muscle repair, glycogen synthesis |
| REM | Rapid Eye Movement | 10–60 min (increases late) | Motor learning, emotional regulation, CNS recovery |

**Key insight**: Slow-wave sleep (SWS) dominates early in the night; REM sleep dominates the final 2–3 hours. Both are essential for athletes:
- Cutting sleep short by 1–2 hours eliminates disproportionately more REM sleep
- Going to bed late reduces SWS time early in the night

## How Much Sleep Do Athletes Need?

| Population | Recommended Duration |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Sedentary adults | 7–9 hours |
| Recreational athletes | 8–9 hours |
| Elite/professional athletes | **9–10 hours** |
| During heavy training blocks | 9–10 hours + optional 20–30 min nap |

**Why athletes need more**: Training stress increases slow-wave sleep demand (the body's repair demand outpaces what 7–8 h provides). Studies consistently show elite athletes are chronically under-slept at 6.5–7 hours actual sleep time.

## Effects of Sleep Deprivation on Performance

### Acute (1 night, <6 hours)
- Perceived exertion at given power output increases ~15%
- Time-trial performance decreases ~3–5%
- Reaction time increases >50ms (equivalent to mild intoxication)
- Mood, motivation, and training drive significantly impaired
- Cortisol elevated, testosterone suppressed

### Chronic (weeks at 6–7 hours)
- VO2max decreases ~5–8%
- Injury risk increases up to 1.7× (Milewski et al., 2014)
- Growth hormone secretion reduced by 30–50%
- Inflammation markers elevated (IL-6, CRP)
- Greater susceptibility to upper respiratory illness

### Sleep Extension (adding 1–2 hours vs. habitual)
- Sprint performance improves 9% (Mah et al. Stanford basketball study)
- Reaction time improves significantly
- Mood and vigor significantly improved
- Pain tolerance increases
- Effect is cumulative over 5–6 weeks of extended sleep

## Hormonal Effects of Sleep

### Growth Hormone (GH)
- ~70% of daily GH secreted during first slow-wave sleep episode (typically 90–120 min after sleep onset)
- GH drives muscle protein synthesis and fat oxidation
- Sleep disruption (late bedtime, alcohol, blue light) delays/reduces GH pulse
- Training heavy and sleeping poorly = impaired recovery

### Cortisol
- Normally lowest at midnight–2am, peaks at 6–8am (cortisol awakening response)
- Sleep deprivation elevates 24-hour cortisol → muscle protein breakdown
- Poor sleep quality elevates resting cortisol even with adequate duration

### Testosterone
- Testosterone restoration primarily occurs during REM sleep (late night)
- 1 week of 5-hour nights reduces testosterone by ~10–15%
- Low testosterone → impaired power output, reduced motivation, slower recovery

### Melatonin
- Produced by pineal gland in response to darkness onset
- Suppressed by blue light (450–495nm wavelength) from screens
- Melatonin onset signals the start of the circadian sleep phase
- Supplemental melatonin (0.5–3mg): effective for jet lag and sleep onset issues; does NOT increase sleep quality if circadian rhythm is already aligned

## Circadian Rhythm and Training Timing

### Performance Peaks
- Physical performance (strength, power, endurance) typically peaks in the **late afternoon (3–6pm)** when:
  - Core body temperature is highest
  - Muscle enzyme activity is optimal
  - Reaction time and coordination are best
- Morning training: performance 3–7% lower than afternoon; warm-up must be longer

### Chronotype Considerations
- **Morning types (larks)**: Natural early risers; perform well in morning training
- **Evening types (owls)**: ~25% of population; natural late-sleepers; suffer when required to train/race early
- For owls forced to race in the morning: gradual chronotype shifting (advance bedtime 30 min/night over 2 weeks) or caffeine strategy

### Travel and Jet Lag
- Eastward travel is harder than westward (harder to advance the clock than delay it)
- Recovery rate: ~1 hour per day for westward, ~1.5 hours per day for eastward
- For races: arrive 1 day early per time zone crossed (budget 3–5 days for major events)
- During jet lag: prioritize sleep over training; use morning light exposure to advance the clock

## Practical Sleep Optimization

### Environment
- **Temperature**: 16–20°C (60–68°F) is optimal; cool room = deeper sleep
- **Darkness**: Complete darkness (blackout curtains or eye mask)
- **Noise**: Consistent white/pink noise or earplugs; traffic noise fragments sleep
- **Screens**: No blue light 60–90 minutes before bed (use night mode or blue-light glasses)

### Pre-Sleep Routine (60–90 min before bed)
1. Dim lights
2. Avoid screens (or use Night Shift / blue light filter)
3. Light stretching or yoga (not vigorous exercise)
4. Cool shower (drops core temperature, accelerates sleep onset)
5. No alcohol within 3 hours (alcohol reduces REM sleep; disrupts second half)
6. No caffeine within 8–10 hours of target bedtime
7. Consistent bedtime ± 30 minutes (even on weekends)

### Nutrition Before Sleep
- **Beneficial**: Casein protein (cottage cheese, Greek yogurt) 30–60 min before bed — slow-release, sustains overnight muscle protein synthesis
- **Avoid**: High-glycemic carbs immediately before bed (glucose spikes disrupt GH release)
- **Controversial**: A small carb snack (100–150 kcal) may reduce cortisol and improve sleep onset for some athletes after hard training days
- **Tart cherry juice**: 250ml twice daily (including before bed) — natural melatonin source, anti-inflammatory; shown to improve sleep duration and reduce muscle soreness

### Napping
- **20-minute nap**: Reduces sleepiness; avoids sleep inertia; safe before afternoon training
- **90-minute nap**: Full sleep cycle; most restorative; schedule in early afternoon; do not nap <4 hours before intended bedtime
- **NASA study**: 26-minute nap improved performance by 34% and alertness by 54% in pilots

## Sleep and Heavy Training Blocks

During training camps, stage races, or high-volume blocks:
- Target 9–10 hours nightly (use blackout environment)
- Add 20–30 min afternoon nap (not after 3pm)
- Monitor sleep quality with wearable (HRV, sleep stages)
- Recognize signs of under-recovery: poor sleep despite fatigue, waking at 3–4am, difficulty falling asleep (paradoxical insomnia from overtraining)

## Sleep Monitoring Metrics

| Metric | Healthy Range | Concern |
|--------|--------------|---------|
| Total sleep time | 8–10h | <7.5h consistently |
| Sleep efficiency | >85% | <80% |
| Deep sleep | 15–20% of total | <12% |
| REM sleep | 20–25% of total | <15% |
| Sleep onset latency | <20 min | >30 min |

## Common Sleep Disruptors for Athletes

1. **Training too late** (within 2–3 hours of bedtime): Sympathetic arousal delays sleep onset
2. **Pre-race anxiety**: Evidence-based CBT-I techniques (stimulus control, sleep restriction therapy) are superior to sleeping pills for chronic insomnia
3. **Alcohol for relaxation**: Reduces sleep quality even when it aids initial sleep onset
4. **Overtraining**: Paradoxically impairs sleep despite fatigue — a key diagnostic sign
5. **Altitude**: Disrupts sleep quality (periodic breathing); acclimatization takes 5–10 days
6. **Over-use of sleeping aids**: Benzodiazepines and non-benzodiazepines reduce SWS quality
