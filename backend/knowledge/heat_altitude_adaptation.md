# Heat and Altitude Adaptation for Cyclists

## Heat Acclimatisation

### Physiological Adaptations

Repeated exposure to exercise in the heat (≥ 35 °C ambient or heat stress achieved
via clothing/sauna) drives a cascade of adaptations over 10–14 days:

| Adaptation | Onset | Magnitude |
|-----------|-------|-----------|
| Plasma volume expansion | Days 3–5 | +5–12% |
| Reduced resting core temperature | Days 5–8 | −0.3–0.5 °C |
- Reduced sweat threshold (sweating begins sooner) | Days 3–6 | — |
| Increased sweat rate | Days 5–10 | +10–20% |
| Lower HR at given workload (heat) | Days 5–10 | −10–15 bpm |
| Increased cardiac output at heat | Days 7–14 | — |

**Plasma volume expansion** is the most important adaptation for cycling
performance: more blood volume means better cardiac filling, higher stroke volume,
and improved muscle oxygen delivery — equivalent in effect to moderate altitude
acclimatisation.

### Heat Training for Sea-Level Performance

Well-controlled studies (Périard et al., 2015; Lorenzo et al., 2010) demonstrate
that heat acclimatisation improves sea-level cycling performance by:
- Increasing VO2max ~5% through plasma volume expansion
- Reducing RPE (rating of perceived exertion) at submaximal intensities
- Improving time-trial performance by 4–8% in temperate conditions

**Protocol**: 60–90 min of moderate-intensity riding (Zone 2) in hot conditions
(≥ 35 °C ambient or with extra clothing to increase thermal load) on 10–14
consecutive or near-consecutive days. Intensity should be manageable — heat stress
itself is the stimulus, so Zone 2 is sufficient and safer than high-intensity heat
sessions.

### Practical Heat Acclimatisation Methods

1. **Passive heat exposure (sauna)**: 20–30 min post-exercise sauna at 80–90 °C
   on 5–6 days/week. Peer-reviewed evidence (Scoon et al., 2007) found 3 weeks of
   post-exercise sauna bathing improved 5 km run time by 1.9% and blood volume by
   ~7% in trained runners. Transferable to cycling.
2. **Hot baths**: 40–42 °C water immersion for 30–40 min post-exercise — lower
   intensity than sauna, more accessible, similarly effective for plasma volume
3. **Overdressing during training**: cycling with extra layers in temperate weather
   increases thermal load without requiring a hot environment

### Racing in the Heat

- **Pre-cooling**: applying ice vests, cold towels, or ingesting an ice slurry
  (−1 °C, 7 ml/kg) 30 min before racing lowers pre-exercise core temperature by
  ~0.5 °C, extending the time before heat exhaustion and improving performance
  by 2–3%
- **Hydration**: begin well-hydrated; target 400–800 ml/hour during racing
  depending on sweat rate and ambient conditions. Electrolyte replacement
  (sodium 500–1000 mg/L) is critical for events > 2 h to prevent hyponatraemia
- **Pacing adjustment**: in high heat, target power/HR targets should be lowered
  by 5–10% relative to temperate conditions; cardiac output is competing between
  working muscles and skin blood flow for thermoregulation

## Altitude Training and Adaptation

### Acute Altitude Effects on Performance

At altitude, reduced atmospheric pressure decreases the partial pressure of oxygen
(PO₂), reducing arterial oxygen saturation (SpO₂) and therefore VO₂max:

| Altitude | SpO₂ (approx.) | VO₂max reduction |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|
| Sea level (0 m) | ~99% | baseline |
| 1,500 m | ~97% | ~3–5% |
| 2,000 m | ~95% | ~6–10% |
| 3,000 m | ~92% | ~15–20% |
| 4,000 m | ~87% | ~25–30% |

**Rule of thumb**: VO₂max declines approximately 1% per 100 m above 1,500 m.
Absolute power at threshold drops proportionally; training intensity targets must
be adjusted.

### Chronic Altitude Adaptations

Given 3–4 weeks at altitude (≥ 2,000–2,500 m), the body adapts through:
1. **EPO (erythropoietin) secretion**: begins within hours; peaks at days 2–3
2. **Red blood cell mass increase**: takes 3–4 weeks; RBC volume rises 5–8%
3. **Improved oxygen delivery**: total haemoglobin mass and haematocrit increase
4. **Ventilatory acclimatisation**: increased breathing rate and tidal volume

### Live High, Train Low (LHTL)

The gold-standard altitude protocol for elite endurance athletes:
- **Live at** 2,000–3,000 m to maximise EPO stimulus and red cell mass increase
- **Train at** < 1,200 m (or sea level) to maintain training quality — at full
  altitude, the reduced VO₂max means athletes cannot reach the absolute power
  outputs needed for effective high-intensity training
- **Duration**: minimum 3–4 weeks; 4–6 weeks for maximal haematological benefit
- **Return to sea level**: RBC mass benefits persist for 3–4 weeks; optimal
  competition window is 2–21 days post-descent (Wilber et al., 2007)

### Altitude Tent / Hypoxic Exposure

Normobaric hypoxic tents (sleeping in simulated altitude via nitrogen dilution)
can partially replicate LHTL:
- Sleeping at simulated 2,500–3,000 m for 8–12 h/night drives EPO secretion
- Evidence suggests ~2–3% improvement in time-trial performance over a 4-week
  camp (Robach et al., 2006)
- Less effective than natural altitude (normobaric vs. hypobaric hypoxia
  differences; lower compliance; no environmental cooling effect)

### Training Prescription at Altitude

Athletes training at altitude (e.g., a 2,500 m training camp) should:
- **Reduce absolute intensity targets**: drop power targets by the same % as the
  altitude-driven VO₂max reduction. At 2,500 m (~10% VO₂max drop), a 300 W FTP
  athlete should target ~270 W for threshold work
- **Increase recovery**: fatigue accumulates faster at altitude; add an extra
  rest day per week compared to sea-level training blocks
- **Maintain Z1–Z2 volume**: easy riding is less affected by altitude; base volume
  can be maintained more readily than high-intensity work
- **Monitor SpO₂**: a resting SpO₂ < 90% signals excessive altitude stress —
  consider descending 200–300 m or reducing training load

### Common Mistakes

- Arriving at altitude and immediately doing high-intensity work — this maximises
  fatigue and injury risk before acclimatisation occurs
- Leaving altitude < 48 h before a key event (you will still be "flat" from the
  acclimatisation process; the optimal window is 3–21 days post-descent)
- Inadequate iron intake: altitude EPO response requires iron to build new
  red blood cells — a ferritin < 30 µg/L can blunt the haematological response
