# Heat and Altitude Adaptation for Cyclists

## Part 1 — Heat Acclimatisation

### Physiological Responses to Heat Stress

Cycling in hot environments (> 25 °C ambient or high humidity) stresses the
cardiovascular system because blood must be redistributed to the skin for cooling,
competing with the working muscles for cardiac output. Key acute responses:

- **Cardiovascular strain**: Heart rate rises 5–10 bpm for the same power output
  compared with cool conditions; cardiac output is partly diverted to cutaneous
  vessels for thermoregulation.
- **Plasma volume contraction**: Sweat rates of 0.5–2.5 L/h cause progressive
  dehydration and haemoconcentration, reducing stroke volume.
- **Core temperature rise**: Performance declines when core temperature exceeds
  ~38.5–39.5 °C; voluntary exhaustion typically occurs near 40 °C.
- **RPE inflation**: Heat independently elevates perceived effort at a given power,
  even before significant dehydration.

### Heat Acclimatisation Protocol

Repeated exposure to heat triggers powerful adaptations within 5–14 days:

| Adaptation | Time course | Magnitude |
|------------|-------------|-----------|
| Plasma volume expansion | Days 3–5 | +5–15% |
| Reduced resting core temperature | Days 5–8 | –0.2–0.4 °C |
| Lower HR at same workload | Days 5–10 | –8–15 bpm |
| Earlier/greater sweat rate | Days 5–14 | +10–25% |
| Lower sweat sodium concentration | Days 7–14 | Reduces electrolyte loss |

**Recommended protocol** (Racinais et al., 2015; Périard et al., 2021):
- 60–90 min of moderate exercise (50–60% VO2max) in heat (35–40 °C) daily
- 10–14 sessions produce near-complete acclimatisation
- Sessions can be passive (sauna, hot bath post-exercise) if active heat training
  is not feasible; passive methods are about 60–70% as effective

**Heat retention for temperate racing**: Adaptations decay within 1–4 weeks.
Time heat acclimatisation to end ≤ 7–10 days before a target race in hot
conditions to maximise retained benefit.

### Pre-Cooling Strategies

Reducing core and skin temperature before a hot event can improve performance
by preserving the body's "heat storage capacity":

- **Ice vest / cold towels** (pre-event): Most practical; reduces skin temperature
  and perceptual heat strain.
- **Cold beverage ingestion** (ice slurry): Lowers core temperature 0.3–0.5 °C;
  shown to improve TT performance by 3–4% in hot conditions (Ross et al., 2011).
- **Cold-water immersion**: Highly effective (core T drop ~0.5 °C) but logistically
  demanding; best reserved for controlled stage-race scenarios.

### Hydration in Heat

- Aim to limit body mass loss to < 2% (beyond this, VO2max and power output
  measurably decline).
- Pre-hydrate: urine should be pale yellow before starting.
- On the bike: 500–800 mL/h in cool conditions; 750–1200 mL/h in hot conditions
  (adjust for individual sweat rate).
- Electrolytes (sodium 500–1000 mg/L) are essential in efforts > 90 min to
  prevent hyponatraemia and maintain plasma osmolality.

## Part 2 — Altitude Adaptation

### Physiological Effects of Altitude

Reduced partial pressure of oxygen (hypoxia) at altitude triggers both acute
impairment and chronic adaptive responses:

**Acute (hours to days)**:
- ↑ Ventilation rate (hypoxic ventilatory response)
- ↑ Heart rate at same exercise intensity
- ↓ Maximal cardiac output
- ↓ VO2max (approximately −1% per 100 m above 1500 m)
- ↓ Blood and muscle pH (respiratory alkalosis initially)

**Acclimatisation (days to weeks)**:
- ↑ Erythropoietin (EPO) secretion → ↑ red blood cell mass and haemoglobin
  concentration (significant effect takes 3–4 weeks)
- ↑ Capillary density in muscle
- ↑ 2,3-DPG (improves O₂ release from haemoglobin at tissues)
- Gradual normalisation of HR and RPE

### Performance at Altitude

Power output for a given effort decreases at altitude because of reduced oxygen
availability. Approximate power decrements for well-trained cyclists:

| Altitude | Expected power decrease (sustained effort) |
|----------|-------------------------------------------|
| 1500 m | ~3–5% |
| 2000 m | ~6–8% |
| 3000 m | ~12–15% |
| 4000 m | ~20–25% |

Cyclists should reduce target watts proportionally when training or racing at
altitude; maintaining the same absolute power would push them into a higher
relative physiological zone than intended.

### Altitude Training Strategies

**Live High, Train High (LHTH)**:
- Traditional approach: reside and train at 2000–3000 m.
- Benefit: haematological adaptations.
- Drawback: reduced absolute training intensity (lower power/speed), which may
  limit neuromuscular development.

**Live High, Train Low (LHTL)**:
- Reside at altitude (2000–2500 m) but descend to lower elevation for quality
  sessions.
- Maximises haematological stimulus while preserving training intensity.
- Considered the gold standard for elite athletes; evidence supports ~2–5%
  improvement in sea-level endurance performance (Stray-Gundersen et al., 2001).

**Intermittent Hypoxic Exposure (IHE) / Intermittent Hypoxic Training (IHT)**:
- Short sessions (1–3 h) in normobaric hypoxia (tents, masks, altitude chambers).
- Evidence is mixed; erythropoietic stimulus requires sustained exposure (> 12 h/day).
- Mainly useful for pre-acclimatisation before a race at altitude rather than
  for haematological benefit.

### Practical Altitude Camp Planning

- **Minimum effective dose**: ≥ 3 weeks at ≥ 2000 m to achieve meaningful
  haematological adaptation.
- **Timing**: Complete the camp 2–3 weeks before the target event to allow
  re-acclimatisation to sea level and recovery from altitude fatigue ("altitude
  bounce" in performance may appear within 2–4 weeks post-camp).
- **Iron status**: Altitude-stimulated erythropoiesis demands adequate iron;
  check ferritin (target > 50 µg/L) before and during altitude camps and
  supplement if indicated.
- **Training load**: Reduce intensity in the first 5–7 days at altitude to avoid
  overreaching; gradually reintroduce quality sessions as acclimatisation proceeds.

### Altitude Sickness

Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS) risk increases above 2500 m:
- Symptoms: headache, nausea, fatigue, poor sleep.
- Prevention: gradual ascent (≤ 500 m/day above 2500 m), adequate hydration,
  avoiding alcohol, acetazolamide (Diamox) if medically prescribed.
- If AMS symptoms appear, descend immediately; do not continue training.
