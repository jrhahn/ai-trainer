# HRV-Guided Training

## What Is Heart Rate Variability?

Heart Rate Variability (HRV) is the beat-to-beat variation in the time interval between consecutive heartbeats (R-R intervals on an ECG). Despite what the name implies, a **higher HRV is better** — it reflects a healthy, responsive autonomic nervous system.

A perfectly regular heart rate (like a metronome) indicates a nervous system that cannot adapt. Healthy variation means the heart is responding dynamically to breath, blood pressure changes, and autonomic signals.

## The Autonomic Nervous System Basis

HRV is primarily regulated by the **autonomic nervous system (ANS)**:

| Branch | Effect on Heart | HRV Effect | When Dominant |
|--------|----------------|------------|---------------|
| Parasympathetic (vagus nerve) | Slows heart; increases variability | ↑ HRV | Rest, recovery, sleep |
| Sympathetic | Accelerates heart; reduces variability | ↓ HRV | Stress, hard training, illness |

**High HRV** (high vagal tone) = parasympathetic dominance = recovery state, ready to train hard  
**Low HRV** (low vagal tone) = sympathetic dominance = residual fatigue, illness, overtraining, psychological stress

## Key HRV Metrics

### RMSSD (Root Mean Square of Successive Differences)
- The gold-standard HRV metric for **short-term recordings** (60s–5min)
- Reflects vagal tone directly
- Less affected by breathing rate than other metrics
- Used by most HRV apps (HRV4Training, Elite HRV, Whoop, Garmin, Polar, Apple Watch)
- **Unit**: milliseconds (ms); typical values 20–100ms depending on fitness and age
- Higher RMSSD = better recovery

### SDNN (Standard Deviation of Normal-Normal intervals)
- Reflects both short-term (vagal) and long-term (circadian, hormonal) HRV
- Used in clinical contexts (24-hour Holter monitoring)
- Less practical for daily athletic monitoring

### HRV Score (Normalized Log Scale)
- Apps like HRV4Training and Whoop normalize RMSSD to a 0–100 scale using natural log transform
- This compression makes changes more comparable across individuals
- Formula: **ln(RMSSD) × 20** (approximately)

## How to Measure HRV Correctly

### Protocol (Morning HRV — Most Evidence-Based)
1. **Same time every morning** — before getting out of bed
2. **Supine position** (lying flat) — HRV is position-sensitive
3. **Relaxed, normal breathing** — do NOT control breathing during measurement
4. **Duration**: 60–300 seconds (longer = more reliable)
5. **Before coffee, food, or significant movement**
6. **Consistent emotional state** — deep breaths to calm down before starting if woken abruptly

### Hardware
- **Polar H10 chest strap**: Gold standard for accuracy; connects to HRV apps via Bluetooth
- **Polar H9**: Similar quality, slightly less features
- **Apple Watch**: Acceptable for trends; less accurate for absolute RMSSD values
- **Whoop, Garmin, Oura Ring**: Convenient but measure during sleep; less protocol-controlled
- **Fingertip apps (e.g., HRV4Training's camera mode)**: Validated vs. chest strap; acceptable for daily use

## Establishing Your Personal Baseline

HRV is highly individual — a value of 40ms may be excellent for one person and very low for another. **Personal baselines are the only meaningful comparison**:

### How to Build a Baseline
- Measure daily for **4–6 weeks** without major changes to training
- Calculate 7-day rolling average → this is your **baseline HRV**
- Also track 7-day rolling average resting heart rate (rHR)
- Note: HRV fluctuates significantly day-to-day; trend and deviation from baseline matter more than single measurements

## Interpreting HRV Scores

### Standard Interpretation Framework

| Relative HRV vs. 7-Day Baseline | Interpretation | Recommended Action |
|----------------------------------|----------------|--------------------|
| >1 SD above baseline | Very recovered | High-intensity training safe; good day for key sessions |
| Within normal range (±1 SD) | Normal recovery | Proceed with planned training |
| 0.5–1 SD below baseline | Slightly reduced | Proceed, but monitor; reduce intensity if planned session is very hard |
| >1 SD below baseline | Poor recovery | Reduce load; replace hard session with easy ride or rest |
| Very low + elevated rHR (+5+ bpm) | Possible illness/overtraining | Rest; do not train through it |

### Combined HRV + Resting HR
Using both metrics increases interpretation accuracy:
- **HRV down + rHR up**: Strong recovery stress signal (fatigue, illness)
- **HRV down + rHR normal**: Moderate fatigue, lifestyle stress, or poor sleep
- **HRV up + rHR down**: Excellent recovery state
- **HRV very high + rHR very low**: Supercompensation; peak performance state

## Research Evidence for HRV-Guided Training

### Kiviniemi et al. (2007, 2010) — Key Studies
- Recreational endurance athletes followed HRV-guided training vs. pre-planned programs
- HRV-guided group performed **significantly better** in VO2max tests and 3km time trials
- HRV group had better performance despite **not necessarily training harder** — they trained smarter
- Conclusion: Adjusting training based on daily readiness outperforms rigid periodization

### Plews et al. (2013, 2014) — Elite Rowers
- 7-day rolling average HRV tracked in elite rowers over 77-week period
- Clear drops in HRV preceded illness (by 2–4 days)
- Normal HRV despite high training load predicted performance maintenance
- Trend (7-day average) was more predictive than single-day measurements

### Buchheit (2014) — Review
- HRV-guided training most effective when:
  - Athlete has established 4+ weeks of baseline
  - Morning protocol is consistent
  - Trend (not single day) guides decisions

## HRV-Guided Programming Protocol

### Step 1: Categorize Each Day
Each morning, check your HRV score against your rolling baseline:
- **Green**: ≥ normal range → execute planned hard session
- **Amber**: Slightly below → proceed but at lower end of prescribed intensity; skip second interval set if legs don't respond
- **Red**: >1 SD below → convert to easy/recovery ride or full rest

### Step 2: Weekly Structure Adjustments
| HRV Pattern (Last 7 Days) | Adjustment |
|---------------------------|------------|
| Consistently green | May add extra intensity day; reduce rest |
| Consistently amber | Reduce weekly TSS by 10–15%; no more than one hard day this week |
| Multiple red days | Recovery week regardless of schedule |
| Rapid decline over 3+ days | Suspect illness or overtraining; see coach |

### Step 3: Long-Term Trend Monitoring
- **Rising weekly average HRV over months**: Good response to training; adaptation occurring
- **Stable HRV despite increasing load**: Athlete tolerating load well
- **Falling weekly average despite reduced load**: Warning sign; may indicate non-functional overreaching
- **Seasonal low** (common in summer heat or at altitude): Adjust expectations; normal

## HRV and Training Periodization

### Base Phase
- HRV typically stable; large aerobic volume tolerated
- Watch for accumulated fatigue in weeks 3–4 of build
- Use 3:1 build:recovery ratio or let HRV guide adaptation weeks

### Intensity/Build Phase
- HRV more variable due to hard sessions
- 24–48 hours post-VO2max session: expect HRV suppression → rest day
- Pattern: Hard day → low HRV → easy day → HRV recovery → next hard day

### Peak/Taper Phase
- HRV should trend upward as load drops
- If HRV doesn't recover during taper: athlete was in deeper fatigue hole than planned
- A rising HRV trend 5–10 days before race = excellent sign of readiness

## Lifestyle Factors That Affect HRV

| Factor | Effect on HRV | Notes |
|--------|--------------|-------|
| Alcohol (1–2 drinks) | ↓↓ (lasts 24+ hours) | Even 1 drink noticeably lowers RMSSD |
| Poor sleep (<7h) | ↓ | Dose-dependent |
| Psychological stress | ↓ | Work, relationship stress shows up in HRV |
| Heat/humidity | ↓ | HRV lower in hot weather; adjust expectations |
| Illness | ↓↓ | Often detects illness 24–48h before symptoms |
| Altitude | ↓ (initially) | Normalizes with acclimatization |
| Meditation/breathwork | ↑ | Even 5–10 min daily practice raises baseline |
| Aerobic fitness improvement | ↑ (long term) | Well-trained athletes have higher HRV baselines |
| Aging | ↓ (natural decline) | Compare to own historical baseline, not population norms |

## Common HRV Monitoring Mistakes

1. **Comparing to others**: HRV is only meaningful relative to your own baseline
2. **Reacting to single-day readings**: Use 7-day rolling average for decisions
3. **Overriding consistently**: If you always ignore low HRV, you lose the benefit
4. **Measuring after getting up**: Must be done immediately upon waking, before rising
5. **Inconsistent timing**: HRV is time-of-day sensitive; always measure at same time
6. **Ignoring context**: Low HRV after a very hard race is expected; one data point in context
