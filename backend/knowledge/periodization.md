# Periodization for Cyclists

## What is Periodization?

Periodization is the systematic planning of athletic training to achieve peak
performance at key competitions. It involves cycling through phases of varying
training load, intensity, and focus to maximise adaptation while managing fatigue
and injury risk.

## Macrocycle, Mesocycle, Microcycle

- **Macrocycle**: The full training year (or multi-year for elite athletes)
- **Mesocycle**: A training block of 3–6 weeks focused on a specific adaptation
  (e.g. aerobic base, threshold, peak)
- **Microcycle**: A single week of training — the basic building block of a plan

## Classical Linear Periodization

Traditional periodization (following the model popularised by Tudor Bompa)
moves sequentially from high-volume / low-intensity to low-volume / high-intensity:

1. **General Preparation (Base)**: High volume, low intensity (Z1–Z2).
   Duration: 8–16 weeks depending on the athlete's level.
   Goal: Build aerobic engine, fat oxidation, mitochondrial density.

2. **Specific Preparation (Build)**: Moderate volume, increasing intensity.
   Introduce threshold and sweet-spot work (Z3–Z4).
   Duration: 6–8 weeks.

3. **Competition (Peak)**: Lower volume, high intensity.
   Race-specific workouts, VO2max intervals, taper.
   Duration: 4–6 weeks.

4. **Transition (Recovery)**: Active rest, unstructured riding, cross-training.
   Duration: 2–4 weeks post-season.

## Block Periodization

Block periodization concentrates a single training stimulus into short, focused
mesocycles (2–4 weeks), followed by recovery. This model is popular in elite
endurance sport:

- **Accumulation block**: High-volume Z1–Z2 work, some Z3
- **Intensification block**: Reduced volume, maximal threshold and VO2max work
- **Realisation/Taper block**: Low volume, race-pace specificity

Research (Issurin, 2010) suggests block periodization may be superior to
traditional periodization for well-trained athletes because concentrated loads
drive stronger adaptive responses before fatigue reverses gains.

## Annual Training Plan Structure for Amateur Road Cyclists

| Phase | Weeks | Intensity Focus | Weekly Hours |
|-------|-------|-----------------|-------------|
| Transition | 2–3 | Z1, unstructured | 4–6 |
| Base 1 | 4–6 | Z1–Z2 | 6–10 |
| Base 2 | 4–6 | Z2, some Z3 | 8–12 |
| Build 1 | 3–4 | SST, threshold | 8–12 |
| Build 2 | 3–4 | Threshold, VO2max | 8–11 |
| Peak/Race | 2–4 | Race-specific, taper | 6–9 |

## Overload and Recovery Principles

The **progressive overload** principle states that training load must increase
over time to continue driving adaptation. Practical guidelines:

- Increase weekly Training Stress Score (TSS) by no more than **5–7% per week**
- Follow a **3:1 ratio**: three weeks of progressive overload, one week of
  reduced load (recovery week)
- A recovery week should reduce volume by **30–40%** while maintaining or
  slightly reducing intensity

## Taper

A taper (pre-competition reduction in training load) is essential for achieving
peak performance. Evidence-based taper guidelines for cyclists:

- **Duration**: 7–14 days for most amateur events; up to 3 weeks for grand tours
- **Volume reduction**: 40–60% reduction in training volume
- **Intensity maintenance**: Keep intensity at or near race pace; do not reduce
  intensity significantly
- **Frequency maintenance**: Keep the number of sessions similar; just shorten them
- Expected performance gains from a well-executed taper: 2–3%

## FTP Testing and Zone Updates

FTP tests should be conducted at the start of each mesocycle or after significant
training changes. Testing protocols:

- **20-min test**: Warm up thoroughly, perform a maximal 20-min effort, then take
  95% of average power as FTP estimate
- **Ramp test**: Perform 1-min steps increasing by 20W; FTP ≈ 75% of peak
  1-min power
- Update all training zones after each FTP test to keep workouts appropriately
  challenging
