# Recovery Science for Endurance Cyclists

## Why Recovery Matters

Training stress alone does not improve performance — adaptation occurs during
recovery. The principle of supercompensation holds that after a training stimulus
and adequate recovery, performance capacity rises above the pre-training baseline.
Insufficient recovery leads to residual fatigue, performance stagnation, and
ultimately overtraining syndrome.

## Types of Recovery

### Immediate (0–24 h post-training)

**Nutrition**: The post-exercise recovery window (30–60 min) is critical for
glycogen resynthesis. Research recommends:
- Carbohydrate: 1.0–1.2 g/kg body mass per hour for the first 4 h after
  exhaustive exercise
- Protein: 20–40 g high-quality protein (leucine-rich) within 2 h to initiate
  muscle protein synthesis
- Hydration: Replace 150% of fluid lost as sweat (weigh yourself before and after)

**Sleep**: The single most important recovery modality. During slow-wave (deep)
sleep, growth hormone is released at its highest rate, driving muscle repair,
glycogen resynthesis, and neural consolidation. Aim for 8–9 h per night during
heavy training blocks.

**Active recovery**: Light Z1 riding (< 55% FTP, < 50% max HR) for 30–45 min
enhances blood flow to recovering muscles without adding meaningful training load.
Lactate clears faster with gentle movement than with complete rest.

### Short-Term (1–7 days)

**Training load management**: Chronic Training Load (CTL) and Acute Training Load
(ATL) are key metrics:
- **Form (TSB = CTL − ATL)**: Positive form (fresh) favours performance;
  negative form (fatigued) is normal during heavy training blocks
- Avoid TSB below −30 for extended periods; plan for TSB to approach +5 to +15
  before key events

**Heart Rate Variability (HRV)**: The coefficient of variation in time between
successive heartbeats (RMSSD) is a sensitive marker of recovery status.
Consistent morning HRV measurement can detect accumulated fatigue before
performance decreases. A RMSSD drop of > 10 ms below a 7-day rolling average
suggests the athlete needs an easier training day.

### Long-Term (weeks to months)

**Deload weeks**: A planned recovery week every 3–4 weeks (volume reduced 30–40%,
intensity maintained) is essential to prevent overreaching. Signs that a deload
is overdue: persistent heavy legs, declining motivation, resting HR ≥ 5 bpm above
baseline.

**Off-season transition**: A 2–4 week transition period after the competitive
season with unstructured, low-intensity activity prevents burnout and allows
hormonal and immune system recovery.

## Overtraining Syndrome

Overtraining syndrome (OTS) is a maladaptation to excessive training load without
adequate recovery. Key signs:

- Performance decline lasting > 2 weeks despite reduced training
- Persistent fatigue not resolved by 48–72 h of rest
- Elevated resting heart rate (> 5 bpm above baseline)
- Disturbed sleep
- Mood disturbances (increased irritability, loss of motivation)
- Increased illness frequency

Treatment requires extended rest (weeks to months). Prevention is far preferable;
build recovery days and weeks systematically into the training plan.

## Modalities and Evidence Summary

| Modality | Evidence Level | Benefit |
|----------|----------------|---------|
| Sleep (8–9 h) | Strong | Paramount; no substitute |
| Carbohydrate nutrition | Strong | Essential for glycogen replenishment |
| Protein nutrition | Strong | Muscle protein synthesis |
| Active recovery rides | Moderate | Faster lactate clearance |
| Cold-water immersion | Moderate | Reduces perceived soreness; may blunt adaptation |
| Compression garments | Moderate | Reduces perceived soreness |
| Massage | Moderate | Improves perceived recovery |
| Stretching/foam rolling | Weak | Limited evidence for performance |

**Note on cold-water immersion (ice baths)**: While ice baths reduce perceived
muscle soreness, emerging evidence (Roberts et al., 2015, *Journal of Physiology*)
suggests they blunt the molecular signalling cascade (mTOR, AMPK) driving
strength and aerobic adaptation. Reserve cold immersion for multi-day stage races
where performance in the next day's race outweighs long-term adaptation.

## Practical Recovery Checklist for Cyclists

After every hard training session or race:
1. Eat a carbohydrate-rich meal with protein within 1 h
2. Rehydrate to urine colour pale yellow
3. Sleep > 7.5 h (8–9 h during heavy blocks)
4. Monitor resting HR and HRV each morning
5. Plan the next session intensity based on how you feel; easy days should be
   genuinely easy
